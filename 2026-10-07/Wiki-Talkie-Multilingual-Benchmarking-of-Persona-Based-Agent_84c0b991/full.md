## Abstract

LLMs are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents in social environments, making it critical to study their ability to faithfully simulate human interactions. Central to this is grounding agents in realistic user personas, yet existing datasets rely on fictional personas and are limited to a handful of languages, lack ing the empirical grounding necessary to evaluate behavioral fidelity across diverse populations. We introduce Wiki-Talkie, a multilingual dataset of real-world conversations from Wikipedia Talk pages across five languages spanning two language families: Germanic (German, English) and Romance (Spanish, French, Italian), paired with personas derived from real user communities and encompassing sociodemographic attributes, self-descriptions, and behaviorally grounded interaction traits. Using Wiki-Talkie, we evaluate agent interactional behavior on a next-turn generation task across various persona conditioning strategies. Our evaluation assesses whether agents collectively reproduce the distributional behavioral patterns observed in human discussions. Results show that user’s comment history exemplifying interaction behavior consistently outperforms explicit persona information. In addition, models systematically underproduce negative or extreme sentiments, while over producing references and suggestions, revealing biases toward agreeableness and positivity. Crucially, these patterns hold robustly across languages, with small cross-lingual differences.<sup>1</sup>

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly embedded in real-world social systems, from community moderation on Reddit<sup>2</sup> to experimental platforms like MoltBook<sup>3</sup> where LLMs autonomously interact at scale. These developments position LLMs not merely as tools, but as agents: autonomous actors participating in governance, culture, and social interaction. Central to the design of such agents is the role they are asked to play (Shanahan et al., 2023; Tseng et al., 2024), e.g. the persona, that is, a composite of individual traits such as background facts, language behavior, and interaction style (Liu et al., 2025a). This approach has been shown to improve alignment with real human populations in applications such as personalized dialogues (Liu et al., 2025a), linguistic annotation (Orlikowski et al., 2025), and social simulations (Argyle et al., 2023; Park et al., 2024).

However, existing datasets for modeling personabased conversational agents rely on fictional personas (Smith et al., 2020; Jang et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2022a,b; Yamashita et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025; Lotfi et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2022; Occhipinti et al., 2024), making it impossible to verify whether agent behavior faithfully reflects the opinions, linguistic patterns, and interaction dynamics of the actual human population being simulated. Datasets extracting personas from large-scale media (Zheng et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021) offer greater realism but are limited to Chinese or English. To our best knowledge, there is no resource providing realworld, multilingual dialogues grounded in real personas within a cooperative setting where pragmatic phenomena such as conflict, misalignment, and evolving viewpoints can emerge—precisely the dynamics that characterize high-stakes scenarios.

To fill this gap, we introduce Wiki-Talkie, a multilingual dataset of real-world discussions extracted from Wikipedia Talk pages across five languages, where registered users collaboratively deliberate on article edits. Wiki-Talkie enables multifaceted evaluation of agents in a consensus-oriented discussion scenario, with optional conditioning on personas derived from Wikipedia user pages, and behavioral traits from comment histories. To safeguard user privacy, all identities are anonymized and persona profiles are regenerated in a neutral fashion rather than verbatim copies, ensuring privacy protection while maintaining research utility.

Our evaluation targets what we term popula tion fidelity: whether agents collectively repro duce the distributional behavioral patterns observed in human discussions, as opposed to individual level personafidelity, i.e., whether a single gener ated response faithfully reflects the provided per sona of a specific user. This choice is principled: individual-level fidelity risks enabling faithful sim ulation of specific individuals, raising ethical con cerns around impersonation and lack of consent (Desai et al., 2025), while population fidelity is more tractable and more relevant for applications such as community simulation and policy analy sis. This aligns with an emerging consensus that evaluation of persona-based agents should operate at the population level (Mansour et al., 2025; Rup precht et al., 2025; Fawaz et al., 2026). To assess population fidelity, we frame the task as next-turn generation: given a thread of previous comments, an agent must generate the next comment simulat ing a specific Wikipedia editor. We conduct eval uations with multiple metrics: instance-level text similarity (ROUGE-L and BERTScore), which pro vides individual-level signals averaged across the entire dataset, and population-level interactional behavior, comparing sentiment and dialog act dis tributions (Searle, 1969) between human comments and those generated by agents powered by multi ple state-of-the-art LLMs, across five languages. We study how agents behave when conditioned on different types of persona information, comparing behavioral traits extracted from comment histories against explicit persona descriptions derived from user profiles. Results show that cross-lingual dif ferences are small, consistent with prior work on multilingual LLM evaluation showing strong performance across high-resource languages (Xuan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). We also find that com ment history consistently proves more effective than explicit persona information as a conditioning strategy, suggesting that showing how a persona behaves outperforms telling what a persona is.

Overall, our contributions are: 1) the construction of Wiki-Talkie, a dataset of Wikipedia discussions supporting research on agentic interaction simulation, whose key advantages are: a) multilingual coverage; b) real-world discussions reflecting established communication norms; c) ground-truth persona information linked to discussions; and d) rich contextual information, with discussions paired with articles; 2) the evaluation of agents based on four LLMs under various conditioning strategies across five languages, providing insights into the limitations of current agentic simulation of human cooperative interactions.

## 2 Related Work

Datasets for Persona-based Evaluation Conversational datasets with persona-related attributes vary in source, language, and use case. Crowdsourced datasets dominate, exemplified by PersonaChat (Zhang et al., 2018b), where crowdworkers converse under fictional personas, and its many extensions: BlendedSkillTalk (Smith et al., 2020) adds empathy and knowledge, FoCus (Jang et al., 2022) adds Wikipedia grounding, Multi-Session Chat (Xu et al., 2022a) models longer-term interaction, DuLeMon (Xu et al., 2022b) uses Chinese mutual personas, and RealPersonaChat (Yamashita et al., 2023) collects real persona descriptions in Japanese. Other datasets rely on synthetic generation (Zhao et al., 2025; Lotfi et al., 2023) or scripted content (Chen et al., 2022; Occhipinti et al., 2024), while social media datasets such as PersonalDialog (Zheng et al., 2020) and PER-CHAT (Wu et al., 2021) extract persona signals from large-scale platforms. Most rely on simulated role-play or synthetic generation and cover few languages: although XPersona (Lin et al., 2021) extends PersonaChat multilingually via automatic translation, naturally occurring interaction datasets remain largely restricted to English and Chinese. Critically, no existing resource simultaneously provides: (i) naturally occurring dialogues, (ii) multilingual coverage, (iii) real user-derived personas linked to the conversations, and (iv) a cooperative yet contentious setting where disagreement, negoti ation, and consensus-building emerge. We address this gap with Wiki-Talkie.

LLM Social Behavior A growing body of work documents systematic biases in LLM-generated social behavior. LLMs exhibit sycophantic tendencies, preferring agreement over truthful disagreement (Malmqvist, 2025), and display a positivity bias that suppresses negative or confrontational outputs (Santurkar et al., 2023). Studies using

LLMs to simulate human populations have shown that, while persona conditioning can improve alignment with survey response distributions (Argyle et al., 2023), models often fail to reproduce the full diversity of human opinions and behaviors (Orlikowski et al., 2025). However, these findings are predominantly based on English-only settings, synthetic survey tasks, or opinion elicitation, leaving open the question of whether such biases persist in naturalistic, multilingual, cooperative discussions. Wiki-Talkie addresses this gap by providing a testbed where LLM social behavior can be evaluated against real human interactions across five languages in a collaborative setting with genuine disagreement and pragmatic complexity.

Datasets Derived from Wikipedia Talks Wikipedia Talk pages remain underexplored despite providing large-scale, naturally occurring collaborative dialogue (Johnson et al., 2024). Prior work has used them to study conversational behavior and harmful interactions (Wulczyn et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2018a), dialogue act classification (Ferschke et al., 2012), dispute detection (Wang and Cardie, 2014), sociolinguistics (Prabhakaran and Rambow, 2016), abuse (Cécillon et al., 2020), and links between discussions and edit histories (Jaidka et al., 2021). WikiConv (Hua et al., 2018) reconstructs full conversational histories including edits and deletions, supporting tasks such as preemptive toxicity detection (Karan and Šnajder, 2019). However, these datasets focus on discourse structure, conflict, or toxicity, remain mostly English-centric, and lack persistent persona representations for multilingual evaluation. To our knowledge, Talk pages have not been used to analyze LLM behavior in socially grounded, collaborative settings.

## 3 Wiki-Talkie

We introduce Wiki-Talkie, a multilingual dataset of real-world discussions extracted from Wikipedia Talk pages across five languages spanning two language families: Germanic (German, English) and Romance (Spanish, French, Italian). Wiki-Talkie is built around three core components (see Figure 1): 1) multilingual threads in which users deliberate on article content; 2) user personas from two complementary sources, a self-reported profile derived from Wikipedia user pages (with attributes such as gender, profession, interests, and contribution topics) and behavioral traits inferred from each user’s comment history; and 3) the article context, paired with each thread as it appeared immediately before the discussion began. Together, these components make Wiki-Talkie suited for research on personaconditioned and document-grounded interaction

## 3.1 Dataset Construction

Threads The threads contained in Wiki-Talkie are extracted from raw Wikipedia Talk page dumps.<sup>6</sup> Wikipedia Talk pages are largely unstructured, making clean conversation extraction a challenging preprocessing task (Ferschke et al., 2012; Hua et al., 2018). We exploit standard conventions: MediaWiki heading syntax for topics, usernametimestamp signatures, and colon-based reply indentation, and handcrafted regular expressions to parse threads into flat sequential turns ordered by timestamps <sup>7</sup>. To address extraction errors, we prompted the extractor model to revise threads by correcting turn boundaries and comment attributions, flagging irrecoverable cases for exclusion (prompt reported in Appendix C, Figure 10).

Persona Profiles For each unique user in the extracted threads, we fetched the corresponding Wikipedia user page via the MediaWiki API.<sup>8</sup> User pages are optional personal spaces where editors describe themselves, their interests, expertise, and contributions. We prompted the extractor model to determine whether a page contained relevant biographical or self-descriptive content, excluding pages consisting solely of administrative templates or userboxes (prompt in Appendix C, Figure 15). For pages with substantive content, the model also generated a natural-language profile summarizing all available details, following recent work on LLM-based persona extraction (Zhang, 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Mansour et al., 2025). Because these are self-declared attributes from real users, the resulting personas avoid the stereotypical or synthetic quality of artificially constructed profiles and naturally incorporate sociodemographic aspects, making them valuable for evaluating how such factors influence agent behavior and biases. Alongside the profile, the model extracted predefined attributes in JSON format (e.g., primary editing topics, gender, profession) to support statistical analysis of user subgroups. Users with successfully generated profiles were subsampled to at most 10,000 per language, prioritizing presence of contribution topics, then gender (favoring women and non-binary users to counteract imbalance), then profession. Only threads where all participants belong to the selected subset were retained.

Persona Behavioral Traits From each user’s comments (across up to 50 randomly sampled threads), we extracted behavioral traits using the extractor model, characterizing each user along eight dimensions including (dis)agreement strategy and editing behavior. Each dimension is filled with closed response options only when sufficient evidence is available, accompanied by the model’s reasoning (prompts in Appendix C, Figures 11– 13). Unlike self-declared profiles, these traits are inferred from concrete interactions, making them especially useful for evaluating how behavioral conditioning shapes agent output. A manual quality audit of profiles and traits (faithfulness, coherence, privacy preservation) is reported in Appendix I.

Articles For each thread, the discussed Wikipedia article is retrieved via the MediaWiki API to provide conversational context, using the revision immediately preceding the first comment’s timestamp. This temporal alignment ensures the retrieved content reflects the article state when the discussion began.

Finally, all usernames are anonymized via deterministic hashing to prevent re-identification, and thread and article formatting is cleaned and normalized for consistency. Full details are in Appendix A.2.

## 4 Experimental Setting

Task Wiki-Talkie supports tasks including comment generation, summarization, and quality assessment. Here, we frame the task as next-turn generation: given the preceding comments in a thread, an agent is asked to generate the next comment in the role of the real user who authored it. We always condition on at least one preceding comment (i.e., the first turn is never generated from scratch), and prior context always consists of ground-truth comments. This task formulation was chosen deliberately to enable controlled, comparable evaluation across languages and conditioning strategies, resulting in over 100 experimental conditions (5 languages × 4 models × 5 conditioning methods). Although Wiki-Talkie supports evaluation along several dimensions—including coherence with context, factual grounding in the associated article, and fidelity to persona conditioning—here we focus on interaction behavior of agents.

Data To keep generation costs manageable, we experimented with a subsample of Wiki-Talkie. We selected threads involving users with at least six comments in the corpus, ensuring a minimum fivecomment history prior to the target comment. From these, we sampled up to 5,000 threads per language, maximizing the proportion of comments authored by females up to 50%. Summary statistics are presented in Appendix D.

Conditioning Methods We defined five conditioning methods to investigate how different types of persona information shape agent behavior in interactions: i) base role-play, where the agent is instructed that it is a Wikipedia editor with no additional information; ii) profile, where the agent is provided with the self-reported profile (gender, age, profession, etc.) of the user who produced the target comment; iii) behavior, where the agent is provided with the user’s behavioral traits; iv) behavior+r, same as behavior but including the extractor model’s reasoning; v) history, where the agent is provided with 5-20 comments by the same user (randomly sampled if more are available), enabling indirect conditioning through stylistic and behavioral patterns in prior contributions. The prompts for each method are provided in Appendix C, Figures 14, 15, 16, 17.

Models We run experiments with four state-ofthe-art models spanning both open-source and proprietary systems: Model A (proprietary), Model B, Model C, and Model D (open-weights).<sup>9</sup> All four are widely-used frontier LLMs at the top of their respective open-weights and proprietary classes at the time of writing, chosen to ensure that our findings reflect the behavior of strong, competitive systems rather than artifacts of weak models. Implementation and generation details are provided in Appendix E.

Evaluation Metrics We report text similarity via ROUGE-L (Lin, 2004) for surface lexical overlap and BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020) for semantic similarity, both raw and rescaled against a baseline (rBERT) as recommended.<sup>10</sup> Since generation is open-ended, these are coarse reference points rather than primary criteria.

Our primary focus is interactional behavior. We extract dialogue acts using the taxonomy of Ferschke et al. (2012), developed for Wikipedia Talk pages, considering 10 acts: CRITIQUE, REQUEST, REFERENCE, COMMIT, REPORT, INFORM, ASK, POSITIVE, NEGATIVE, and PARTIAL.<sup>11</sup> Both human and agent comments are annotated with the extractor model as LLM annotator (prompt in Appendix C, Figure 12), which achieves 0.76 F1 against human judgment (Appendix F). Each comment can carry one or multiple acts. We additionally assess overall response tone via sentiment prediction on both human and agent comments.<sup>12</sup>

## 5 Results

## 5.1 Text Similarity

Figure 2 shows ROUGE-L and rBERT scores averaged across models and languages; full results, including average comment length and raw BERTScore (which remains stable across setups, ∼0.84–0.85, and is thus less informative), are in Appendix G, Tables 11–12. While our primary target is population fidelity (Section 4), these instancelevel metrics provide a complementary individuallevel signal.

ROUGE-L scores never exceed 0.154. While low in absolute terms, this is expected given the open-ended nature of the task, where the space of plausible responses is virtually unbounded; scores are thus best read as relative measures. rBERT hovers around zero, indicating that agent-human similarity is broadly comparable to random sentencehuman pairs, further confirming the difficulty of the task.

Across both languages and models, history consistently yields the highest similarity, with

ROUGE-L up to 0.154 (Model A in es) and rBERT up to 0.105 (Model A in de). behavior+r is the second most effective, with consistent gains over base (e.g., ROUGE-L up to 0.138 for Model A in es; rBERT up to 0.085 for Model A in de). By contrast, profile generally degrades similarity relative to base (e.g., rBERT drops on average from 0.053 to 0.040 for Model A, and from −0.017 to −0.049 for Model D), likely because prompting agents to reflect persona traits leads them to incorporate non-relevant details, producing longer and more elaborated responses without improving content alignment. Indeed, agent comments are consistently longer than human comments across all conditions (Appendix G). behavior similarly fails to improve similarity for most models and languages, with only modest gains in some cases (e.g., Model B’s ROUGE-L from 0.078 to 0.082).

Cross-lingually, ROUGE-L scores are consistently higher for es and en than for de and it, partially mirroring human comment length: longer reference texts provide richer surface for lexical overlap. rBERT trends are less pronounced; en falls slightly below the others, largely driven by Model B’s particularly low English scores.

Overall, concrete demonstrations—through comment histories (history) or behavioral traits paired with reasoning (behavior+r)—are more effective than abstract descriptions (behavior) and sociodemographic information (profile) at steering agent output toward human-like text. This is the dominant pattern across models and languages, while cross-lingual differences remain small, though we note that all five languages are high-resource and European. The consistency is nonetheless non-trivial given that they span two language families and differ in Wikipedia community size and editorial norms.

## 5.2 Interaction Behavior

In the following we discuss evaluation of behavioral patterns through dialog act JSD and sentiment WD; detailed scores are reported in Appendix G, Tables 13 and 14.

Acts JSD. Figure 3 presents average JSD scores aggregated across models (left) and languages (right). Scores range between 0.1 and 0.3, indicating only moderate divergence between human and agent dialogue act distributions. history is the strongest method, achieving the lowest JSD overall (0.085 for Model A in en). behavior+r is second, with consistent gains over base (e.g., Model B: 0.272 → 0.241; Model D: 0.199 → 0.166). By contrast, behavior is the weakest, increasing JSD across most models and languages (except Model B, where it improves over base, 0.272 → 0.245). profile yields only marginal effects, with gains limited to Model A (0.150 → 0.147). These trends are broadly consistent across languages, though en achieves the lowest average JSD throughout (e.g., 0.188 under base, 0.175 under behavior+r), likely reflecting English-centric pretraining. Cross-lingual differences, however, remain marginal.

Overall, these results reinforce the pattern observed for text similarity: concrete behavioral demonstrations (history, behavior+r) are consistently more effective than abstract descriptions or sociodemographic factors (behavior, profile) at aligning agent behavior with human distributions, echoing the well-established advantage of few-shot over zero-shot conditioning (Brown et al., 2020).

One might wonder whether this gap reflects a difference in information type or simply in information quantity, since history provides 5–20 raw comments while profile and behavior supply compressed summaries. We address this concern in Appendix H, where we show that performance under profile does not improve consistently with profile length: ROUGE-L and rBERT are flat, and where acts and sentiment JSD change, they mostly worsen with longer profiles. Combined with the observation that profile degrades performance relative to base despite adding tokens, this argues against a pure context-budget explanation and supports our interpretation that the operative factor is information type, not quantity.

Per-act differences To assess whether divergences concentrate on specific labels, we analyze per-label proportion differences (δ) between human and agent distributions, where positive values indicate human over-production and negative values indicate agent over-production. Figure 4 shows differences averaged across languages, omitting acts with negligible variation (ASK, CRITIQUE, POSITIVE, PARTIAL). Differences are computed between proportions rather than raw counts to account for lower act density in agent comments (Appendix G). Full breakdowns are in Tables 13–14.

Agents systematically over-produce REQUEST (up to δ = −0.177 for Model C in de on behavior) and COMMIT (up to δ = −0.108 for Model D in fr on behavior), while humans produce more RE-PORT (δ = 0.088 for Model C in fr on behavior). Together, this suggests that agents tend to operate by requesting and committing to changes, yet report fewer actions already undertaken. INFORM is under-produced by agents across most models, possibly reflecting insufficient factual grounding (Model A and Model D diverge in some methods). REFERENCE shows a model-dependent pattern. NEGATIVE is consistently under-produced (δ from 0.020 to 0.104), consistent with sycophantic tendencies of LLMs (Malmqvist, 2025). Overall, the most systematic biases concern operativity (over-production of REQUEST, COMMIT) and negativity (under-production of NEGATIVE, REPORT), pointing to a consistent tendency for agents to be more action-oriented and less confrontational than humans.

Per-act differences by gender To examine whether agents reproduce dialog act trends equitably across genders, we analyze per-label proportion differences split by gender for two models with contrasting alignment levels (Model A, higher; Model B, lower) and two languages with similar contrast (English, higher; French, lower), under history (best overall), profile (the only method with explicit gender), and base as reference. Differences are shown in Appendix G, Figure 18. Trends are consistent across genders for all labels and methods. Crucially, profile does not behave systematically differently from base or history, indicating that explicit gender conditioning neither amplifies nor reduces gender disparities in dialog act profiles.

Sentiment Sentiment alignment broadly mirrors the dialog act trends: history yields the best alignment (minimum WD 0.097 for Model C in en), followed by behavior+r (0.168 in en). Across languages, en shows the lowest WD (0.315 on average under base) and es the highest (0.452), consistently across methods. As shown in Figure 5 for Model A under history, agent responses rarely exhibit sentiment extremes (very positive or very negative) and contain fewer negative comments than humans, consistent with their lower rate of NEG-ATIVE acts. These patterns hold across models, conditioning methods, and languages (Appendix G, Figure 19). Overall, sentiment converges with the broader finding that agents tend to avoid negative or confrontational content across all conditioning methods.

## 6 Conclusions

We introduced Wiki-Talkie, a multilingual dataset derived from Wikipedia Talk pages to evaluate agent interactions across five languages, using four state-of-the-art LLMs under different persona conditioning strategies. Our evaluation targets population fidelity—whether agents collectively reproduce the distributional behavioral patterns of human communities—complemented by instancelevel text similarity metrics. Our experiments show that grounding agents with concrete comment examples consistently outperforms abstract persona descriptions across all languages. Notably, sociodemographic information can degrade agent performance, an encouraging finding for privacypreserving applications. In addition, agents are consistently more action-oriented and less confrontational than humans, revealing a bias toward agreeableness and avoidance of extremes. Cross-lingual differences remain small, with English showing only slightly greater alignment. Overall, these results underscore both the promise and limitations of persona-conditioned generation, motivating further research into more effective simulation strategies. While we focused on next-turn generation to enable controlled comparison across a large experimental space, Wiki-Talkie is designed to support richer evaluation settings, including multi-turn generation, summarization, factual grounding assessment, and quality evaluation, all of which we encourage the community to explore.

## Limitations

Dataset Construction Despite our multi-stage extraction and revision pipeline, some residual noise remains in Wiki-Talkie, including occasional misattributed comments, imperfect comment boundaries, and formatting artifacts not fully resolved during preprocessing Such noise is inevitable at this scale and is unlikely to substantially affect evaluation or analysis.

Language Coverage Wiki-Talkie currently covers five European languages (English, German, Spanish, French, and Italian), limiting its cultural and linguistic diversity. While these languages span two distinct families (Romance and Germanic) and vary in Wikipedia community size and editorial norms, they are all high-resource; findings may not generalize to language communities with different writing systems, discourse conventions, or editorial practices. Future work should extend Wiki-Talkie to additional languages, particularly those underrepresented in NLP research. The dataset construction pipeline and evaluation framework are fully language-agnostic and straightforwardly extensible to lower-resource and non-European languages.

Dataset Richness The richness and consistency of user personas vary considerably within and across languages. Some profiles contain detailed self-reported attributes, while others are sparse. English is generally better represented in both quantity and quality, meaning persona conditioning experiments may be more reliable for English than for other languages. Moreover, despite subsampling to increase the representation of women and nonbinary users, the dataset remains gender-skewed, which may affect the robustness of gender-based analyses.

Persona Conditioning Our experiments focus exclusively on prompting-based persona conditioning, leaving alternative approaches to future work, such as fine-tuning (e.g., Thakur et al., 2024;

Takayama et al., 2025), retrieval-augmented conditioning (e.g., Huang et al., 2023; Salemi et al., 2024), or embedding-based persona representations (e.g, Liu et al., 2025b).

Information Density across Conditioning Methods Our conditioning methods are not strictly matched on information density: history provides 5–20 raw comments, while profile and behavior supply compressed summaries. We probe this confound in Appendix H by stratifying profile performance by profile length and find no consistent improvement with profile length across four metrics, supporting an information-type rather than information-quantity interpretation. We did not perform the analogous analysis for history length because the conditioning pool is concentrated in the 16–20 comment range, leaving lower-comment buckets too sparse to support a meaningful comparison. A fully matched ablation—e.g., comparing history against equally long synthetic summaries distilled from the same comments—remains a direction for future work.

Scope of Persona Representations Our profiles and behavioral traits are produced by a single anonymized extractor model and a specific prompting design. A manual audit (Appendix I) indicates that these representations are largely faithful and well-grounded, with imprecisions limited to isolated factual details. Nonetheless, our findings about profile and behavior should be read as conclusions about sociodemographic and behavioral descriptors as we generated them, rather than as universal claims about all possible persona representations. Alternative summarization strategies, richer attribute schemas, or different extractor models could in principle yield different results.

Evaluation Scope Our primary evaluation target is population fidelity—whether agents collectively reproduce the distributional behavioral patterns observed in human discussions—rather than individual-level persona fidelity, i.e., whether each generated response faithfully reflects the specific persona provided. This choice is principled: individual-level fidelity risks enabling faithful simulation of specific individuals, raising ethical concerns around impersonation and lack of consent (Desai et al., 2025), while population fidelity is more tractable and more relevant for applications such as community simulation and policy analysis, in line with recent work advocating populationlevel evaluation of persona-based agents (Mansour et al., 2025; Fawaz et al., 2026; Rupprecht et al., 2025). Assessing fine-grained individual-level persona consistency remains a direction for future work.

## Ethic Statement

## Privacy, Data Protection, and Contextual Integrity

Wiki-Talkie is derived from Wikipedia dumps, released under a Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 license. While publicly accessible, we recognize that contributors may reasonably expect their comments to be archived for research purposes but not necessarily used for personaconditioned simulation. Accordingly, we treat this dataset as human-derived data requiring safeguards beyond copyright compliance, guided by the principles of data minimization, proportionality, contextual integrity, and harm reduction.

Dataset Construction and Anonymization The dataset does not contain original user pages verbatim. Instead, we generated neutral, naturallanguage re-elaborations summarizing publicly stated interests and interactional patterns, deliberately omitting usernames, real names, contact information, and external links. This process inherently introduces a layer of abstraction: the resulting profiles are approximate characterizations that may diverge from the original content through omission, rephrasing, or occasional inaccuracy. While this means profiles should not be treated as ground-truth descriptions of actual persons, we acknowledge that the same process may occasionally introduce erroneous attributes, including sensitive ones. We sought to mitigate this risk through manual inspection of a random sample of outputs (see Appendix I).

Usernames are replaced using salted cryptographic hashing, where the salt is stored separately from the dataset and is accessible only to the principal investigator, preventing straightforward reversal or precomputation attacks (see Appendix A.2). Within comment text, we also anonymized mentions of third-party usernames to the extent possible. However, a residual risk remains where usernames appear in non-standard forms or where real names are mentioned in conversation.

Re-Identification Risk and Access Control Complete anonymity cannot be guaranteed when working with public textual data. While salted hashing prevents trivial username reconstruction, a content-based re-identification risk exists: because Wikipedia discussions are publicly archived and indexed, motivated individuals could potentially identify original authors via distinctive phrases or search queries. Our dataset does not introduce new exposure beyond what is already publicly accessible, but we recognize that aggregation and structuring may lower the cost of such searches.

To mitigate these risks, the dataset is released under the CC BY-SA 4.0 license, inherited from the underlying Wikipedia-derived content. Rather than hosting an open public mirror, we distribute it through a lightweight access request in which prospective users identify themselves and their intended use. This provides an audit trail and a point of contact while raising the effort required for indiscriminate bulk access. As a matter of research norms rather than a binding condition, we ask that recipients not attempt to de-anonymize contributors, not use the data to impersonate or manipulate editorial processes, and work from the most recent release.

We provide a dedicated contact address for individuals who wish to request removal of their data. Upon verification, corresponding entries are added to a blocklist and removed in subsequent releases. Our goal is risk mitigation rather than elimination, and we consider the residual risk proportionate to the scientific value of studying collaborative online deliberation at scale.

Informed Consent Individual informed consent was not sought, as contributions to Wikipedia are made under the CC BY-SA license and the Wikimedia Foundation’s Terms of Use, which explicitly permit reuse and derivative works. This is consistent with established practice in NLP research on Wikipedia-derived data (Ferschke et al., 2012; Hua et al., 2018), and the scale of contributors makes individual consent infeasible. Nevertheless, we recognize that licensing permissibility does not fully resolve the ethical tension between contributors original communicative intent and downstream research applications; we address this gap through the layered safeguards described above.

Harmful or Sensitive Content Wikipedia Talk pages may occasionally contain harassment, conflictual exchanges, or incidental personal disclosures. We do not seek to amplify or foreground such content; the dataset is designed to analyze aggregate interaction patterns in collaborative environments, not for reputational analysis of specific contributors. We did not apply active content filtering in order to preserve the ecological validity of the discussions. Access restrictions are intended to further prevent misuse or amplification of harmful material.

## Experimental Ethics and Persona Conditioning

The objective of this research is not to recreate, approximate, or impersonate specific individuals. Persona-conditioned agents are stochastic constructs derived from coarse behavioral summaries, used solely to examine population-level dynamics in simulated group discussions. Although individual-level conditioning is methodologically necessary to capture heterogeneity in interaction styles, all evaluation and reporting are conducted at the aggregate level. We do not evaluate model fidelity to specific users, publish individual outputs, or claim to model the beliefs, intentions, or identities of real contributors.

We recognize that repurposing public discourse for AI research raises novel ethical questions concerning digital representation and contextual integrity. Our ethical justification rests on proportionality: the scientific value of understanding large-scale collaborative behavior, combined with layered safeguards (anonymization, profile re-elaboration, controlled access, opt-out mechanisms), outweighs the residual and mitigated risks.

In Table 2, we show statistics on users and personas, with Figure 7 showing the corresponding word distributions for user pages and profiles. User pages are right-skewed across all languages, with most users having short pages and a minority producing substantially longer ones. English users exhibit the richest descriptions, with both the longest average user pages and profile lengths (98.07 words), and nearcomplete coverage of contribution topics (100%), gender (67%), and profession (66%), reflecting the larger and more active English Wikipedia community. ## A.2 Anonymization and Normalization

All usernames in Wiki-Talkie are anonymized via deterministic hashing with a keyed hash function and salt, ensuring consistent mapping across the dataset while preventing reversal. Username occurrences are identified and replaced not only in structured metadata but also within comment text, where editors frequently mention one another through wikilink syntax or plain text references.<sup>13</sup> Thread formatting is then cleaned and normalized: leading and trailing decorative characters (dashes, equals signs) are removed, HTML line breaks are converted to newlines, styling tags (<div>, <span>, <u>) are stripped while preserving content, <nowiki> and <code> tags are removed but enclosed text is retained, indentation markers are removed, and whitespace is collapsed and trimmed. MediaWiki templates and external links are preserved to maintain fidelity to the original discussions. The same normalization was applied also to articles.

## E Generation Details

All next-turn generations were produced using a cloud-based batch inference service. Generations were conducted with a temperature of 0 to ensure deterministic outputs, and a maximum of 1,024 tokens were generated per response. To accommodate the context window constraints of each model, input prompts were dynamically truncated when necessary: if the combined length of the system and user prompts exceeded the available input budget (i.e., the model’s context window minus the reserved generation tokens), the article body was progressively shortened by iteratively reducing it to 90% of its current length until the prompt fit within the budget, with a <article\_truncated> marker appended to signal the truncation. For the history method, if the full history caused the prompt to exceed the context window, the number of historical threads was progressively reduced until the prompt fit; instances where the resulting history fell below the minimum threshold of 5 threads were flagged as underrepresented. Prompts that could not be reduced to fit within the context window even after exhausting all truncation strategies were discarded.

## F Validation of Acts Annotation

To validate our LLM-based annotation approach, we rely on the SEWD dataset (Ferschke et al., 2012). SEWD provides act annotations for English Wikipedia talk page discussions, a setting analogous to Wiki-Talkie corpus. In SEWD, the reconciled labels of two trained annotators serve as the gold standard. We use these reconciled labels to evaluate the quality of our LLM-as-annotator pipeline (the extractor model). For clarity, we rename and merge some labels to reduce granularity; the full mapping is reported in Table 4.

However, the format of SEWD differs substantially from Wiki-Talkie. In SEWD, label spans are assigned directly to raw comments within a thread, without prior splitting or cleaning, whereas in our scenario we aim to provide the LLM with preceding comments as conversational context and a single target reply to be labelled. To bridge this gap, we first convert SEWD into the Wiki-Talkie format, structuring each instance as a target comment to be annotated along with its preceding comments as context, and aligning the corresponding SEWD labels. This conversion inevitably introduces noise; we therefore manually clean the resulting instances and discard those that could not be reliably mapped. From the original 1,500 threads in SEWD, this procedure yields 735 usable threads, which constitute our validation set.

## G Full Results

Tables 11, 12 and Tables 13, 14 report full results for text similarity and interaction behavior evaluation, respectively. Average BERTScore and ROUGE-L scores are discussed in Section 5.1, while average dialog act and sentiment scores are discussed in Section 5.2. Figure 18 reports the proportion difference between human and agent dialog acts split by gender, and Figure 19 shows the distributions of sentiment scores for human and agent comments, discussed in Section 5.2. In the following, we detail text length and act density ratio, which are not discussed in Section 5.

Text Length Across all conditions, agentgenerated comments are substantially longer than human comments. Human comments average approximately 48–78 words depending on the language (de being the shortest at ∼48 words and es the longest at ∼78), while agent output consistently exceeds these figures, often by a wide margin. Notably, the history method yields the shortest agent comments across all models—for instance, Model A drops to ∼88 words on average, its closest approximation to human length—suggesting that exposure to real interaction excerpts helps calibrate verbosity. Conversely, the behavior and profile methods tend to inflate output length, particularly for Model D and Model B. Interestingly, es also elicits slightly longer agent comments than other languages across all conditioning methods, mirroring the fact that human comments in es are themselves the longest.

Act Density Ratio The act density ratio is consistently below 1.0 across all conditions, indicating that agent-generated comments are uniformly richer in communicative intents than their human counterparts. The lowest ratios are observed under the behavior method, where agents appear to enact the prescribed behavioral traits, which are dense in prescriptive interactional information. By contrast, when grounded with real examples via history, the ratio rises above 0.7 on average across all models and languages, with the exception of Model B, suggesting that concrete exemplars help bring act density closer to human levels.

## H Information Density vs. Information Type

A natural concern with our experimental design is that the comparison across conditioning methods may not be fully information-balanced: the history method receives 5–20 raw prior comments, whereas profile and behavior receive compressed summaries. Part of the gain observed under history could therefore reflect a larger context budget (i.e., information quantity) rather than a strictly better form of persona representation, i.e., information type.

To probe this confound, we examine how performance varies with the amount of conditioning information provided. We focus this analysis on profile length, where the available token budget varies meaningfully across users (see Table 2 and Figure 7). For history, the analogous analysis would require comparing performance across different numbers of prior comments. However, the history sampler defaults to using up to 20 comments per user, and in practice the vast majority of conditioning pools fall in the 16–20 range; lower-comment buckets are too sparsely populated to support a meaningful comparison. We therefore restrict the analysis to profile length, which provides a well-distributed and interpretable proxy for input richness.

We stratify all experimental instances by profile word count into five buckets (1–50, 51– 100, 101–150, 151–200, 201–250) and compute, for each bucket and for each language–model pair, the four metrics used in our main analysis: ROUGE-L, rBERT, dialog act JSD, and sentiment JSD. Buckets with fewer than 20 samples are omitted, so de has no 201–250 bucket and es no 151– 200 bucket. The es 201–250 bucket is dominated by a single user, who contributes 45 of its 67 samples, so its values should be read as largely individual rather than bucket-level. Results are shown in Figures 20–23. The red dashed lines indicate the proportion of samples falling in each bucket; trends at the longest bucket should be interpreted with caution due to sample sparsity.

Findings Across all 20 language–model pairs, none of the four metrics improves consistently as profile length grows. A pure context-budget account would predict a downward slope for JSD metrics (lower divergence = better alignment) and an upward slope for similarity metrics (higher = better) as more conditioning tokens are provided. ROUGE-L and rBERT are nearly horizontal in every configuration (within-pair ranges of at most 0.026 and 0.044, respectively). Where the JSD metrics do move, they mostly move in the opposite direction: dialog act JSD rises for all four models in fr, mainly from the 151–200 bucket onward, and sentiment JSD rises with profile length for all four models in es, fr, and it. The few downward trends are small and confined to en and de: dialog act JSD for Model A in en decreases from 0.135 to 0.098, and sentiment JSD decreases for some models in en and de, in en largely because of the sparse 1–50 bucket (under 3% of samples).

Taken together, these results provide evidence against an information-density explanation for our main results. Together with the observation reported in the main text—that profile degrades performance relative to base despite adding tokens—this analysis supports our interpretation that information type, not information quantity, drives the differences across conditioning methods. We acknowledge that this analysis does not fully isolate every possible confound (e.g., compressioninduced loss of stylistic exemplars), and we discuss this further in the Limitations.

## I Qualitative Audit of Profiles and Behavioral Traits

Because profiles and behavioral traits are produced automatically by an LLM and then used as the key conditioning signals for profile, behavior, and behavior+r, their quality directly affects the interpretation of downstream results. To complement the quantitative analyses in the main text, we conducted a manual audit of these representations along three dimensions: (i) relevance and faithfulness of generated profiles with respect to the source user pages, (ii) coherence between assigned behavioral traits and the supporting reasoning produced by the extractor model, and (iii) privacy preservation, i.e., whether sensitive information (real names, usernames, contacts) is leaked into the generated representations. We sampled 10 users per language (50 total) uniformly at random from the experimental subset and one of the authors inspected, for each user, the generated profile, all 8 behavioral traits with their reasoning, and the underlying user page.

Profile faithfulness In all 50 cases, profiles contain information that is relevant to and supported by the source user page. We identified a small number of imprecisions, none of which substantially distort the persona:

• English: one numerical drift (“42 years” instead of “45 years”); one rounding error on edit count $( ^ { \ast } 1 0 0 , 0 0 0 ^ { \cdot }$ instead of “100,000k”);

• French: one case where contributed-article information was omitted;

• German: one case where the username was incorporated into the profile as if it were a separate entity, likely due to hallucination triggered by user page ambiguity;

• Italian: one ungrammatical fragment (“Ha adottato il giorno 16 luglio e il comune di Plesio”) reflecting parsing ambiguity in the user page.

We additionally observed that information from userboxes—small standardized templates that Wikipedia editors place on their user pages to declare interests, languages spoken, locations, or affiliations—is sometimes incorporated into the profile and sometimes ignored, with no clear pattern across languages. Moreover, we noticed that gender, when not explicitly self-declared, is typically inferred from gender-marked terms (especially in the four grammatical-gender languages: German, Spanish, French, and Italian) and, when available, from given names.

Behavioral trait coherence For each of the 8 behavioral categories, we checked whether the assigned trait value is consistent with the accompanying reasoning. Out of $5 0 \times 8 = 4 0 0$ trait-reasoning pairs, in only 7 cases (across English, Spanish, and Italian) was the assigned label found to trace back to the provided reasoning. In most other cases, the reasoning explicitly cites concrete excerpts from the user’s comment history in support of the assigned trait, providing transparent grounding for the conditioning signal.

Privacy The audit surfaced two cases that warrant explicit mention:

• one English profile mentioning a middle name, which the user themselves had self-disclosed anecdotally on their user page;

• one German profile reproducing the username as if it were a separate named entity (the same hallucination case noted above).

No real legal names, contact details, or other directly identifying information were leaked into the generated representations.

Implications for downstream results The audit indicates that the generated profiles and behavioral traits are largely faithful and well-grounded, with imprecisions concentrated at the level of isolated factual details rather than systematic distortions. This argues against an interpretation in which the relatively weak performance of profile and behavior is driven primarily by representation quality. At the same time, the audit highlights that profiles vary substantially in the kinds of information they emphasize (e.g., uneven uptake of userbox content) and that the compression step inherent in summarization may lose stylistic exemplars present in the raw history signal. In this sense, our results should be read as conclusions about the conditioning representations as we generated them, rather than as universal statements about all possible sociodemographic descriptors. We acknowledge this scope explicitly in the Limitations.
